# When the AI writes the code, the evidence is what you actually read

*The spoken edition of The Working Copy for 2026-11-26. Narrated by Uncle Burt.*

When an AI writes the code, what actually counts as evidence that it works? That is the question a recent Stack Overflow blog post puts on the table, and it is a question a lot of us have been quietly avoiding. The old habit was to glance at the diff, trust the vibes, and ship. That habit was shaky even when a human wrote every line by hand. Now the code arrives faster than you can possibly read it, and the phrase "it ran once" is doing a lot of load-bearing work it was never designed for.

The post frames the problem right there in its title: when AI writes the code, what counts as evidence? Notice what the question assumes before it even gets to an answer. It assumes that generated code is not self-certifying. A green test run, a successful build, an assistant that confidently says it is done — none of those are evidence in the way a careful reviewer would accept from a human colleague. They are outputs. Outputs can be deeply convincing while being completely wrong, and the convincing part is exactly what makes them dangerous.

Here is the part nobody prints on the box: this was already true. Code review has always been a claim about evidence. I read this, and here is why I believe it does what it says it does. What changed with generated code is throughput. When code arrives faster than you can read it, your reading becomes the bottleneck by design. And the temptation, when you are the bottleneck, is to skip the reading and then call the skipped reading a workflow improvement. That is a shrug wearing a lanyard. It looks official. It is not.

The honest move, the one the page lands on, is not to stop reading. It is to make your reading cheaper and more targeted, so that the limited attention you actually have goes where it matters. That is the whole premise of the technique in the written edition: a read-only inspection pass you run against a repository before you trust anything in it. You interrogate the project with three questions, and none of them require you to change a single file. It is reconnaissance, not renovation. Evidence first, edits later, if ever.

Question one: where does execution actually start? Entry points are where the code stops being a pile of text and starts being a program. If you do not know what runs first, you do not know what you are reviewing. Question two: what does the project claim about itself, in its own documentation? The docs are the story the code is telling about itself, and stories are where discrepancies begin. Question three: where is the mass? The biggest files are usually where complexity, risk, and the fifteen things nobody mentioned all live together in one place. Not literally fifteen, probably. But you know the feeling.

You can answer all three questions without touching anything, and that is deliberate. The inspection in the written edition is read-only by design. It lists the entry points a manifest or main file reveals, surfaces the documentation, and ranks the largest files so you know where your attention should go first. It stops before editing anything on purpose. Evidence collection and modification are different jobs, and mixing them is how reviews quietly become rubber stamps. An inspector that can also rewrite is an inspector you will eventually stop watching. Like a coworker who refactors while you are mid-sentence. Eventually you just let them.

So concretely, here is how the worked example goes. Point the inspection at a repository an agent just generated or modified. You get an inventory back in seconds — the entry points, the stated purpose, the heavyweight files — before any of that code executes. That inventory is your evidence-gathering pass. Nothing has run. Nothing has been trusted. You are holding a map of the territory, drawn from the territory, which is more than the chat window will ever hand you, because the chat window only shows you the story. The story is never where the bodies are buried. The map at least shows you the cemetery.

The inventory earns its keep when it disagrees with the story. If the entry points include a script nobody mentioned, that is a finding. If the biggest file is something the assistant cheerfully described as a small helper, that is a finding too. What you have discovered is the gap between what you were told and what actually exists, and that gap is precisely where generated code goes wrong. It is also invisible from the outside of a chat window, which is the whole reason the inspection exists rather than, say, asking the agent how it went and taking the answer as gospel. The agent, remember, is the same party that called the four-hundred-line helper small.

Now, an important line to draw, because the page draws it carefully too. The Stack Overflow blog post supplies the question and the argument about evidence. The three-question inspection technique is the written edition's answer to that question, not something the blog post itself prescribes. The source gives you the why; the edition supplies the how. When you are weighing what to trust, keep that distinction in your head, the same way you would keep the distinction between a spec and an implementation. Both can be useful. Only one of them is the code.

The source also cannot establish specifics about your repositories, your agents, or how much faster generation has gotten in any measurable sense. What it can establish is the framing: code now arrives faster than review can absorb it, and outputs — green builds, confident messages, passing runs — are not the same thing as evidence. That framing is enough to act on, and honestly, it is more than enough to lose sleep over, if you are the person whose name is on the merge. If your name is not on the merge, someone else is losing that sleep on your behalf, which is its own kind of finding.

Once you have the inventory, treat it as a reading order, not a verdict. Start with the entry points, because that is what will actually run when someone presses the button. Skim the documentation to see what the project promises. Then check the biggest files against those promises. This is not a heroic deep read of every line. It is triage. You are spending your scarce attention where the code is actually heavy, instead of reading the fun parts and skimming the parts that will wake you at three in the morning. Every codebase has both. The fun parts are bait.

If the docs and the code disagree, believe the code, and update your mental model — or send the agent back with a specific, file-level instruction. Specific beats general, and it is not close. Asking the assistant to explain what a function does before you run it produces evidence. Asking it to make it better produces more code, which is the one thing you already have too much of. The general instruction is a wish. The specific instruction is a question, and questions are how you catch things. Wishes are how you end up with a second draft of the same surprise.

There is a rhythm worth noticing here, because it generalizes past this one tool. Gather evidence read-only. Compare it to the story you were told. Send back specific corrections. Repeat. That loop works whether the code came from an agent, a colleague, or your past self at an hour your present self would rather not discuss. The loop does not care who wrote the lines. It cares whether anyone read them, and review was always, at bottom, a claim that somebody did.

Your next step is small, and the page means it: run the inspection on the last repository an agent touched for you, today, and compare the output to what you remember being told about it. If the two match, you have earned a small amount of real confidence, which is worth more than a large amount of borrowed confidence. If they do not match, congratulations, you have just caught something a green checkmark would have cheerfully waved through. That is the whole job of review, whether a human wrote the lines or a very confident autocomplete did.
